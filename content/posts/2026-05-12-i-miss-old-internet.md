# I Miss the Old Internet

There is a specific kind of silence I associate with the old internet.

Not literal silence, of course. Dial-up modems screamed. Message boards refreshed slowly. Banner ads blinked. Guestbooks filled with usernames like `dragon_wolf_87` and `starrygirl2001`. Embedded MIDI tracks played automatically, often against everyone’s will. Nothing about it was polished or quiet in the modern sense.

But beneath all of that noise, there was room.

Room to wander. Room to be weird. Room to learn slowly. Room to build something small and imperfect and still feel like it mattered. Room to ask a question without turning yourself into a personal brand. Room to help a stranger simply because you knew the answer and remembered what it felt like not to.

I miss that internet.

I miss the internet before every page felt optimized, before every platform trained us to perform, before every post was weighed against engagement, reach, monetization, and the possibility of being misunderstood by thousands of people who were never the intended audience.

I miss the old internet because it felt less like a marketplace and more like a neighborhood.

## The Internet Used to Feel Handmade

One of the most beautiful things about the old internet was how obviously human it was.

You could land on someone’s personal website and instantly know that a real person had spent an unreasonable number of evenings building it. The colors might clash. The navigation might be confusing. There might be a spinning skull GIF in the corner for no clear reason. The font might be lime green on a black background. Half the links might be broken.

And yet, it had soul.

People made little websites about everything. A page dedicated to tropical fish. A shrine to a favorite video game character. A collection of homemade guitar tabs. A guide to repairing a specific model of old camera. A fan site for a forgotten sci-fi show. A page about bread baking, ham radio, antique computers, medieval armor, fountain pens, houseplants, toy trains, local history, or one person’s extremely detailed ranking of every boss in a role-playing game released in 1998.

These were not content strategies. They were offerings.

Nobody was asking, “What is my niche?” in the cold, algorithmic way we ask it now. People just had interests. They had obsessions. They had things they loved enough to explain to strangers for free. Their websites were messy little museums of enthusiasm.

That mess mattered.

A handmade website told you, “Someone lives here.” It had fingerprints. It had strange side pages and personal notes and outdated links and images saved at the wrong resolution. It was not trying to be frictionless. It was trying to exist.

Today, so much of the web looks like it was poured into the same mold. Clean layouts. Infinite feeds. Identical profile pages. Uniform buttons. White space. Rounded corners. Cards. Metrics. Follow buttons. Subscribe buttons. Sign up modals. Cookie banners. “You may also like.”

It is easier to use, maybe. But it often feels harder to love.

## Forums Were the Campfires

Before everything became a feed, forums were where people gathered.

They were slower than social media, and that was part of their strength. A forum thread could last for years. Someone would ask a question in 2006, another person would reply in 2008, a third person would revive the thread in 2012, and some desperate traveler from the future would arrive in 2024 and whisper, “Thank you, this fixed my problem.”

Forums had memory.

They were not perfect. They had drama, gatekeeping, flame wars, cliques, bad moderators, and users who treated every beginner question like a personal insult. The old internet was never a utopia. Nostalgia can sand down the rough edges if we let it.

But forums also created something that modern platforms struggle to reproduce: shared context.

You recognized usernames. You learned who knew what. You knew which member had restored five motorcycles, which one was the Linux wizard, which one gave brutally honest writing feedback, which one always posted thoughtful replies at 3 a.m., and which one would inevitably derail the thread with a joke but somehow still provide the right answer.

There was reputation, but it was not always numerical. It was earned through presence.

People helped each other in forums because the forum itself felt like a place. You were not shouting into a global stream. You were walking into a room where the same people might be there tomorrow. That changed the way you spoke. It made patience easier. It made accountability harder to escape.

On a good forum, asking for help felt like approaching a workbench where a few people were already leaning over some problem, sleeves rolled up, coffee nearby, saying, “Alright, show me what you tried.”

That spirit was everywhere:

- Tech forums where someone would walk you through a driver issue step by step.
- Car forums where people documented repairs with blurry photos and heroic patience.
- Writing forums where strangers critiqued your short story with more care than some paid editors.
- Game forums where players mapped secrets, built mods, wrote guides, and argued passionately about balance patches.
- Health, parenting, craft, and hobby forums where people exchanged hard-earned knowledge long before everything became a short-form tutorial.

The best forums were archives of generosity.

They were proof that people like helping. Not always for clout. Not always for money. Sometimes because knowing something creates an urge to share it. Sometimes because someone once helped them. Sometimes because the problem is interesting. Sometimes because community is built one useful reply at a time.

## Helping People Was Part of the Culture

One thing I miss most is how common it felt to help a stranger online without needing to convert that help into a product.

Someone would ask how to fix a line of HTML. Someone else would explain it. Someone would ask how to beat a difficult level. Someone would write three paragraphs and include a map. Someone would ask why their computer kept crashing. Someone would ask for the specs, the operating system, the last thing they installed, and then patiently troubleshoot until the issue was solved.

There was pride in being useful.

Not every answer was right, and not every helper was kind. But there was a culture, especially in hobby spaces, where knowledge wanted to move. People made FAQs because they were tired of repeating themselves, yes, but also because they cared enough to preserve the answers. They wrote tutorials because documentation was bad or nonexistent. They uploaded patches, templates, save files, scans, scripts, mods, and guides because they wanted the next person to have an easier time.

The internet was full of tiny ladders left behind by people who had already climbed the wall.

That is one of the old internet’s greatest legacies: the idea that ordinary people could document ordinary knowledge and make it globally useful.

A teenager could write a tutorial that helped thousands. A retiree could build the best resource on a niche historical topic. A hobbyist could explain a repair better than the manufacturer. A fan could maintain a database more lovingly than any official company ever would.

Nobody needed permission. Nobody needed a media company. Nobody needed a polished studio setup. You could just make the page.

That freedom changed lives.

## Passion Was the Search Engine

Search used to feel more like exploration.

You would follow a link from a forum signature to a personal page, then from that page to a links section, then from there to a webring, then to another person’s site, then to a half-finished archive last updated in 2003. It felt like wandering through side streets.

The journey was inefficient, but inefficiency is not always a flaw. Sometimes inefficiency is how discovery happens.

Modern platforms are very good at giving us more of what they think we want. The old internet often gave us what someone else loved.

That distinction matters.

When a person curates a links page, they are saying, “Here are the things I think are worth your time.” When an algorithm curates a feed, it is often saying, “Here are the things most likely to keep you here.” Those goals can overlap, but they are not the same.

Old websites had blogrolls, resource pages, link exchanges, webrings, buttons, badges, and “cool sites” lists. These were not just navigation tools. They were social maps. They showed relationships between people, interests, and communities.

A personal website could be a doorway into a whole world.

And because so many sites were maintained by people with no commercial motive, you could stumble into knowledge that had not been flattened into “content.” It did not always begin with a hook. It did not always have a thumbnail. It did not always tell you to like and subscribe. It just existed because someone cared.

## The Beauty of Small Audiences

The old internet was smaller, or at least it felt smaller.

A post did not have to be for everyone. A forum reply did not need to survive mass misinterpretation. A blog could be written for thirty regular readers. A personal update could be meant for friends, not an invisible public. A hobby site could serve the few hundred people on Earth who also cared about that exact thing.

Small audiences create different writing.

You can be more specific. You can be more sincere. You can take longer to explain. You can use in-jokes. You can assume some shared context. You can build trust over time instead of fighting for attention in the first three seconds.

The modern internet often collapses every audience into one giant crowd. Friends, coworkers, strangers, critics, brands, bots, journalists, relatives, and enemies can all become part of the same imagined readership. That changes what people say. It makes everyone more guarded and more performative at the same time.

On the old internet, you could have a corner.

A corner is not the same as a platform. A corner has edges. It has regulars. It has a tone. It can be decorated. It can be neglected for a while and returned to. It can be strange without immediately being dragged into the center.

I miss corners.

## Before Everything Became Content

The word “content” has done something unfortunate to the way we think about creativity.

A guide, a diary entry, a photo, a joke, a tutorial, a review, a poem, a rant, a drawing, a recipe, and a carefully maintained archive all become “content,” as if they are interchangeable units of online material flowing through distribution channels.

The old internet had plenty of bad writing, spam, ads, and nonsense. But it was easier to feel the difference between making something and producing content.

Making something begins with curiosity, delight, frustration, memory, obsession, or the desire to connect.

Producing content often begins with the question, “What will perform?”

That question is not evil. People deserve to be paid. Creators deserve audiences. Good work can exist inside modern platforms. Some of the best educational material ever made is being made right now.

But when performance becomes the atmosphere around everything, it changes the work. It changes the creator. It changes the audience. Suddenly every hobby looks like a potential brand. Every interest becomes a funnel. Every opinion becomes a post. Every vacation becomes a photo opportunity. Every project becomes a launch.

The old internet gave us more room to do things badly in public.

That was important. Badly is how many good things begin.

Your first website could be ugly. Your first tutorial could be clumsy. Your first fanfic could be melodramatic. Your first essay could ramble. Your first forum post could ask something obvious. The stakes were lower, so participation felt easier.

A web full of amateurs is not a lesser web. Amateur means lover. The old internet was built by lovers of things.

## The Loss of Ownership

Another thing I miss is the feeling of ownership.

A personal website was yours in a way a social media profile is not. You could organize it however you wanted. You could make the homepage a manifesto, a scrapbook, a directory, a joke, or a disaster. You could add pages that no algorithm would ever recommend because they were not designed to be recommended. You could link outward freely. You could move at your own pace.

On modern platforms, we rent our identities inside someone else’s architecture.

The landlord decides what the room looks like. The landlord decides which doors are visible. The landlord decides whether your friends see your updates. The landlord can change the rules, bury your posts, remove features, inject ads, prioritize video, deprioritize links, or shut down the whole building.

We gained convenience. We lost a certain kind of sovereignty.

The old internet asked more of us technically, but it also gave more back. Learning basic HTML was not just a skill; it was a form of agency. View source, copy, paste, break something, fix it, upload, refresh. That loop taught people that the web was not magic. It was made. And because it was made, you could make part of it too.

That realization was powerful.

## Imperfection Made It Human

I miss under-construction pages.

I miss “last updated” timestamps.

I miss guestbooks filled with awkward compliments.

I miss hand-drawn buttons that said “email me.”

I miss forum signatures with quotes, pixel art, and too much personal information.

I miss avatars people used for ten years.

I miss usernames that became familiar before you knew anything else about the person.

I miss the feeling of finding a page that had not been updated in a decade but still contained the exact answer you needed.

I miss websites where the owner wrote, “Sorry I haven’t updated in a while, life got busy,” as if the internet were a garden and the visitors were neighbors passing by the fence.

So much of today’s web is smoother. It is also more temporary. Stories vanish. Feeds bury things. Search results decay. Platforms rise and fall. Posts are optimized for the moment and then swallowed by the next moment.

The old internet decayed too, but its ruins felt different. A dead personal website could still feel like a preserved room. A dead platform feels more like a demolished mall.

## The Old Internet Was Not Innocent

It is important to say this clearly: the old internet had serious problems.

It could be exclusionary, cruel, chaotic, and unsafe. Harassment existed. Scams existed. Bigotry existed. Misinformation existed. Technical barriers kept many people out. Communities could become toxic. Moderation could be arbitrary or nonexistent. Nostalgia should not turn the past into a cartoon paradise.

But missing something does not mean denying its flaws.

What I miss is not every part of the old internet. I do not miss slow downloads, broken layouts, malware, pop-ups, or the particular terror of accidentally making a phone call impossible because someone was online.

What I miss is the spirit of a web that felt more participatory than consumptive.

I miss the sense that the internet was something we made together, not just something delivered to us through a handful of enormous platforms.

I miss when more people felt like citizens of the web rather than users of apps.

## Can We Get Any of It Back?

I think we can, at least in pieces.

Not by pretending it is 2004 again. The world has changed. The internet is bigger, faster, more commercial, and more deeply woven into daily life. There is no simple return.

But we can revive some old habits.

We can write on personal blogs again, even if only a few people read them. We can make little websites for our hobbies. We can join forums, or build forum-like communities, and treat them as places rather than engagement machines. We can answer questions generously. We can document what we learn. We can link to other people’s work. We can maintain archives. We can resist turning every interest into a brand.

We can make pages that are not trying to go viral.

We can create for the person who is searching at midnight, frustrated and almost ready to give up.

We can write the guide we wish we had found. We can leave the helpful comment. We can preserve the obscure fix. We can build the tiny fan page. We can share the reading list. We can start the thread. We can thank the stranger who solved the problem. We can be the stranger who solves it for someone else.

The old internet was not great because of its technology. The technology was often terrible.

It was great because people treated the web as a place to contribute.

That is the part worth saving.

## A Small Web Is Still Possible

Maybe the internet became too big to feel like a neighborhood. Maybe the platforms won too much of our attention. Maybe search got worse, feeds got louder, and everything became a little too optimized for extraction.

But the small web is not gone.

It is still there in personal blogs, independent forums, open-source communities, fan archives, newsletters, niche wikis, tiny directories, handmade pages, and quiet corners where people still gather around shared interests. It is harder to find than it should be, but it exists.

Every time someone makes a website just because they love something, the old internet flickers back on.

Every time someone answers a beginner kindly, the old internet flickers back on.

Every time someone writes a tutorial with no paywall, keeps a forum alive, shares a weird collection, maintains a resource list, or links to another person’s project simply because it deserves to be seen, the old internet flickers back on.

Maybe that is what I really miss: not the old internet as a period of time, but the old internet as a posture.

A way of being online that says:

This does not have to scale.

This does not have to monetize.

This does not have to impress everyone.

This can just be useful, or interesting, or heartfelt, or strange.

This can just be mine.

This can just be for us.

I miss the old internet. But more than that, I miss who we were when we believed the web was ours to build.

And maybe, quietly, stubbornly, one small website at a time, it still can be.
